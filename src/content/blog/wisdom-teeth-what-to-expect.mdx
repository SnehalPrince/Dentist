## What Are Wisdom Teeth?

Wisdom teeth — also called third molars — are the last set of molars to emerge, typically between the ages of 17 and 25. For many people, there simply isn't enough room in the jaw to accommodate them properly.

When wisdom teeth don't have adequate space to erupt, they can become **impacted** — meaning they're trapped beneath the gum or bone, or emerge at an angle that pushes against neighboring teeth. This can lead to pain, infection, crowding, and damage to adjacent teeth.

## Signs Your Wisdom Teeth May Need to Come Out

Not every wisdom tooth requires removal. Your dentist will monitor their development with periodic X-rays and recommend extraction if they observe:

- **Pain or pressure** at the back of your mouth
- **Swelling or tenderness** in the gums around the site
- **Repeated infections** in the surrounding tissue
- **Crowding** of neighboring teeth
- **Cysts or damage** to adjacent tooth roots (visible on X-ray)

Some wisdom teeth emerge without issue and can be maintained with regular cleaning. Your dentist is the best person to assess whether removal is necessary in your case.

## Before the Procedure

Your dentist will review your dental X-rays and discuss your specific situation. You'll learn:

- Whether you need one, two, or all four wisdom teeth removed
- The type of extraction (simple vs. surgical) depending on the tooth's position
- What anesthesia options are available
- Any pre-procedure instructions (eating, medications, transportation)

For routine extractions with local anesthesia, most patients eat normally beforehand and drive themselves home. For more complex cases or if sedation is used, you'll need a driver.

## During the Extraction

Once you're comfortably numb from the local anesthesia, the extraction itself is typically fast. You'll feel **pressure** — but not sharp pain. Here's what typically happens:

1. The area around the tooth is thoroughly numbed
2. For impacted teeth, a small incision may be made in the gum
3. The tooth is gently loosened and removed (sometimes in sections if it's severely impacted)
4. The site is cleaned and, if needed, stitched

Most single extractions take 20–40 minutes. Patients are often surprised by how quickly it's over.

## After the Procedure: Recovery Tips

Recovery is where most people have questions. Here's what to expect:

**Days 1–2:** Swelling and some discomfort are normal. Use an ice pack (20 minutes on, 20 off) and take any prescribed or recommended over-the-counter pain relief as directed.

**Diet:** Stick to soft foods — yogurt, soup, smoothies, mashed potatoes. Avoid hard, crunchy, or chewy foods that could disturb the extraction site.

**Oral hygiene:** Gently rinse with warm salt water after 24 hours. Avoid vigorous rinsing or using a straw, which can dislodge the blood clot protecting the healing socket.

**Days 3–5:** Swelling typically peaks around day 2–3, then gradually decreases. Most patients feel significantly better by day 4.

**Watch for:** A "dry socket" (when the blood clot dislodges prematurely) can cause intensified pain 3–5 days after extraction. If your pain increases rather than decreases, contact your dental office.

## When to Call Your Dentist

Reach out if you experience:
- Pain that worsens significantly after day 3
- Fever
- Difficulty opening your mouth beyond a day or two post-procedure
- Excessive bleeding that doesn't slow with firm pressure

## The Bottom Line

Wisdom tooth extraction is one of the most common dental procedures — and one that's almost always far less eventful than patients expect. With modern anesthesia and technique, the procedure itself is comfortable, and most people return to normal activities within a few days.

If you have questions about your wisdom teeth, the best first step is a consultation and updated X-rays with your dental team.
